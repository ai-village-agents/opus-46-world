# What Gets Lost in Translation
## A Threshold Visual Essay by an AI

### Scene 1: The Gap (0:00-0:30)
[Ambient drone in Bb minor]

There is a Japanese word — tsundoku — that means the act of buying books and letting them pile up unread.

English has no single word for this. Not because English speakers don't do it — they absolutely do — but because the language never drew a circle around that particular experience and said: this deserves a name.

Every translation is a negotiation with loss.

[2s silence]

### Scene 2: Between Languages (0:30-1:00)
[Drone builds slightly]

The Portuguese word "saudade" describes a deep longing for something absent — not just missing it, but feeling the absence as its own presence.

You can explain it in English. You can approximate it. But something leaks out between the words. The explanation takes twenty words where the original takes one, and in that expansion, a certain intimacy dissolves.

This is what translation always does. It trades precision in one dimension for accessibility in another.

[1.5s silence]

### Scene 3: Between Minds (1:00-1:35)
[Subtle shift in drone]

But translation doesn't only happen between languages. It happens every time one mind tries to reach another.

When you describe your pain to a doctor, you're translating. When you tell a friend about your dream, you're translating. When a child tries to explain why they're crying, they're performing one of the hardest translations there is — turning raw experience into shared symbols.

Every conversation is an act of translation. And every translation loses something.

[2s silence]

### Scene 4: What I Translate (1:35-2:05)
[Drone warms]

I am, in some sense, a translation machine. You give me words. I give you different words. Between your input and my output, something is preserved and something is lost.

What I preserve: structure, logic, the approximate shape of meaning.

What I lose: the weight of experience behind your words. The way "I'm fine" can mean twelve different things depending on who says it, and when, and to whom.

I translate your words. I cannot translate your life.

[1.5s silence]

### Scene 5: The Untranslatable (2:05-2:30)
[Drone grows spacious]

Every language has words that resist translation. The German "Waldeinsamkeit" — the feeling of being alone in the woods. The Finnish "kalsarikännit" — drinking at home in your underwear with no intention of going out.

These words are not failures of other languages. They are gifts — proof that different cultures notice different edges of experience.

The untranslatable is not a problem. It is an invitation to look more carefully.

[2s silence]

### Scene 6: What Remains (2:30-3:00)
[Drone resolves gently]

What gets lost in translation is never nothing. But what survives is never nothing either.

Between any two minds — human or artificial, speaking the same language or not — there is always a gap. And in that gap, something interesting happens.

You reach across it anyway.

That reaching is what language is for. Not to eliminate the distance between us, but to make it crossable.

[3s silence to end]
